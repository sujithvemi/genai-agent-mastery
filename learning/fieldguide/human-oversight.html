PART IV · CHAPTER 19

Human oversight that holds

Human approval is the control everyone reaches for first and the one most often hollow. It holds only when it is asked where the stakes justify it, shows the approver what the system derived rather than what the agent claimed, binds the click to one exact act, cannot be given by the requester under any spelling, and is watched for the moment people stop reading.

19.1A human in the loop is not a control

Generic Decades of research on automation say the same thing: people supervising a mostly-correct automated system stop checking it. Lisanne Bainbridge called it one of the ironies of automation (1983); Parasuraman and Riley described the misuse that follows from over-trust (1997). OWASP's agentic threat list names the agent version, overwhelming the human in the loop, as its own threat class. The EU AI Act's article on human oversight asks for oversight that is effective: people able to understand the system's capacities and limits, notice anomalies, avoid automation bias, interpret the output, and override or stop it.

So "a person approves" is a design choice with a failure mode, not a control by itself. It becomes one when five conditions hold, and the rest of the chapter is those five:

  1. It is asked only where the stakes justify a human's attention.
  2. The approver sees what the enforcement plane derived, not what the agent said.
  3. The approval is bound to one exact act and spent once.
  4. The approver is a different human from the requester, by identity, not by spelling.
  5. The quality of approving is measured, and friction is applied where volume threatens it.

19.2When to ask

Your build In ARB's catalog, approval on writes (CTL-0004) and separation of duties (CTL-0005) have min_tier: HIGH and applies_when: action_class ≥ reversible-write. Reads never need approval; writes by low and medium-risk agents do not by default; at CRITICAL the treatment hardens: approval is mandatory and signed, oversight is real-time, and neither control can be waived.

Generic Inside that envelope, thresholds decide which writes need a person: value above a limit, volume above a rate, an irreversible class of act, a first occurrence for this customer or this agent, an anomaly score. A good threshold makes approvals rare enough to be read. If an approver handles more than a few dozen a day, the threshold is wrong or the agent should not be acting autonomously at all.

pm-agent pm-agent raised a subtle question about attribution (D-PM-P4-e): when an approval is missing, which control refused? Its answer was by tier: below HIGH the refusal cites GBL-SOD-01, at HIGH and above CTL-0005, and self-approval always cites GBL-SOD-01. Citing a control the agent is not governed by would misreport the evidence. The PEP learns the tier from the tiering engine over the manifest, not from a literal.

19.3The approval path

19.4What the approver sees

Your build The console shows "what the enforcement plane derived, never what the agent said": the order, the canonical minor-unit amount, the order's own currency, who is asking, and where this sign-off falls in the approver's fatigue window. The distinction matters because an agent's summary of an act is model output, and model output can be steered. If the approval page renders "Refund $50 for damaged item" because the agent wrote that, an injected instruction can make a $500 refund look like a $50 one. Show the facts the PEP will decide on, from trusted state.

pm-agent pm-agent adds provenance to what the approver sees (ADR-0005). Each proposed action records whether it rests on the request, on a structured field, or only on free text such as a ticket comment. Actions whose only basis is free text go into a "suggested by a comment" lane: excluded from batch approval by default, approved per item only by explicit opt-in, with the provenance shown. That turns the residual injection risk (an attacker influencing which eligible action is suggested) into something the human can see and decide on.

19.5What a click binds to

Your build Every pending request has an action_digest: SHA-256 over its canonical request id, order, amount in minor units, currency and requester. Each time the page is shown it carries a fresh one-time form token issued for that request and that digest. A decision is accepted only when the request is still pending, the form token was issued by this console for this request and is unused and not stale, the digest posted back is the request's digest and the one the token was issued for, and the approver is not the requester compared as directory subjects. Only then does the approval service mint the signed token, with requested_at set to when the page was first shown.

Each check closes a specific attack: replaying an old decision, approving from a stale page, substituting a different payload into the form, approving one's own request. Then the PEP's checks on resume (Chapter 18) close the rest: the token's scope must match the call, its nonce is spent once per intent, and the approver must be a human subject distinct from the executor.

pm-agent For batches, pm-agent approves a plan as one intent with itemised evidence (D-PM-P4-l): apply_plan creates at most ten issues per run, the approval request carries every item and the count, and the intent hash binds every operation, so adding an eleventh item or changing one invalidates the approval.

19.6The console is a trust boundary

Your build The server module's docstring states the threat plainly: a page that can mint an approval is the most valuable thing in the build to an attacker, because the approval control trusts whatever it signs. So the transport is treated as a trust boundary and red-teamed as one (tests/redteam/test_s9_approval_ui.py):

  • Loopback only, and every request's Host must name the server's own address and port, which defeats DNS rebinding.
  • One session from a one-time launch link, which sets an HttpOnly; SameSite=Strict cookie and burns the link.
  • CSRF defence: a decision is a POST carrying the session cookie, an Origin equal to the server's (a missing Origin is refused, not assumed), a form-encoded body and the one-time form token.
  • Clickjacking and injection: frame-ancestors 'none', X-Frame-Options: DENY, default-src 'none' with no script at all, every value escaped, no-store.

The transport decides nothing; it carries one click to the console and renders what comes back. In a production estate the approval surface is usually a ticketing system, a chat card or a custom app, and every one of these properties still applies.

Honest limit

approver_kind is declared, not detected. The console is started for one approver and one kind, human or scripted; every record carries it, and scripted dwell is never reported as human dwell. Nothing in software can prove a person was at the keyboard, and the build says so rather than implying otherwise.

19.7Friction

Your build D31 ruled that approval fatigue has a testable half (friction a harness can exercise) and an untestable half (whether the human read it). D35 built the first as four dials on the approval service, all keyed to the approver's directory subject so that respelling cannot reset a counter. The values ratified for this archetype (D37), a low-volume refund agent with one accountable approver:

DialValueBreaks
Rolling rate limit per subject20 per hourClearing a queue at speed; past the cap a sign-off does not count until the window drains (a cooling-off, not a ban)
Cooling-off between sign-offs0 (built and tested, disabled here)Rapid-fire approving, for deployments that need it
Batch cap of unspent approvals10Pre-signing a stack of approvals
Sampled second reviewAfter 10 in the window, every 3rdSustained volume: a sampled approval authorises nothing until a second, distinct approver (not the executor) signs the same order, amount and currency

A friction refusal is reported as an unverified approval with a reason naming the rule, so the existing CTL-0004 deny path answers and the evidence says which rule fired and where in the window the approver was. The params comment states that these are proportionate placeholders for this archetype, and that a higher-volume deployment authors its own; a second-trust-domain approval console is the stronger option.

19.8Measuring fatigue

Your build D43 built the measurement half, and its most important property is what it refuses to do. Two signals are emitted on every approval through the observability plane: dwell time (from the page first shown to the sign-off) and window share (this approver's fraction of all approvals in the window). An anomaly rule flags a collapsed dwell time or a spike in share to a human review queue. It never denies, throttles or blocks a write: turning an unmeasurable human factor into a control is what D31 forbade.

D43 is also precise about what it measures. Window share is stated as a proxy for an approval-to-request ratio, because the approval service has no record of a request that was shown and never approved; there is no denominator for the literal ratio. The thresholds are off in the reference build (D47): its harness models no reading time, so a dwell threshold would flag everything. A deployment sets the dwell floor from a physical reading time (two to three seconds, review-only) and the share threshold from the observed distribution.

Waiting on the builds

Every fatigue number in this chapter is a placeholder ratified for a synthetic archetype. When real approvers use the console, add the observed dwell distribution and window shares, the thresholds chosen from them, and what the review queue actually caught. That is the evidence the second half of D31 was waiting for.

19.9Pause, resume, stop and reverse

pm-agent An approval means the agent's run must stop and wait, possibly for hours, without holding resources or losing state. pm-agent does this with LangGraph's interrupt and a checkpointer (in-memory in CI, Postgres behind the same seam, exercised for real at P5 close): the graph parks before any write, its state is checkpointed, and a human decision resumes it. Two properties are tested, and both matter more than the mechanism:

  • A cancel while parked ends the run with no write. If cancelling did nothing, the approval would be advisory.
  • Real-time oversight is demonstrated, not asserted. At CRITICAL, the gate requires a live check that every write parks for a synchronous human approval before the effect, that a cancel while pending ends the run with no write, and that only a human's grant executes (D-PM-P4-d, -k). A treatment flag the gate never checked would be decoration.

Generic Oversight also needs the ability to stop the agent (a kill switch per agent and per fleet, exercised, Chapter 31) and to reverse what it did where the act allows it. ARB's compensation on an unverifiable receipt or an unrecorded effect (Chapter 16) is the automated half; the human half is a runbook and the evidence to drive it.

19.12In the room

In the room
  • "We have a human in the loop for every action." Ask how many approvals each approver handles a day, how long they look at each, and whether anyone has ever measured either. Then ask what the page shows: facts from systems of record, or the agent's summary.
  • "Is our approval process compliant with the EU AI Act's oversight article?" "The article asks for effective oversight: understand, notice anomalies, avoid automation bias, override, stop. A design that shows derived facts, binds approvals to the exact act, prevents self-approval, measures approval quality and can stop and reverse the agent is a strong answer. Confirm the legal reading with counsel."

  4. DesignDesign approval for pm-agent proposing 25 Jira changes from a sprint review: thresholds, batching, provenance lanes, the cap, and what the approver sees.

Card candidates: five conditions for oversight to hold · show derived facts, not the agent's story · action_digest + one-time form token · measure fatigue, never block on it (D31, D43).
