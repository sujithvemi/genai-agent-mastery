PART VII · CHAPTER 45

Across platforms: capability map and governance

A large enterprise does not choose one agent platform; it ends up with several. This chapter puts the platforms side by side on one capability map, says where each leads and lags as of October 2026, and then gives the architecture for governing across them: centralise the rules, the registry and the evidence; federate enforcement to each platform's native points and to your own gateways; normalise what comes back; and prove, with tests, that the layers decide the same way.

45.1No platform sees the others

Generic Each platform's governance stops at its edge: GEAP's registry lists GEAP agents, Agent 365 lists the Microsoft tenant's agents, AWS Agent Registry lists what is registered in AWS, and SaaS vendors list their own. Several now offer to register "agents from anywhere" (Foundry Control Plane, ServiceNow AI Control Tower, IBM's control plane), but registration is voluntary and enforcement still happens platform by platform. A CISO's question, "which agents can reach customer data, and who approved them?", spans all of them.

Two rows are empty across the board, and they are the core of your reference build: tamper-evident decision evidence and sign-off bound to configuration. These are the gaps a client framework and toolkit must fill on any platform (Chapter 53).

45.3Centralise, federate, normalise, prove

  1. Centralise what must be one thing: the catalog of controls and their semantics, the tiering, the registry of record, the evidence store and the release gate.
  2. Federate enforcement: for each control and each platform, name the enforcement point, native where it meets the control's contract, your gateway or PDP where it does not (the mapping tables in the previous three chapters).
  3. Normalise what comes back: inventory, decision logs and traces, into one schema with provenance labels.
  4. Prove the layers agree: run the same golden and adversarial cases through every enforcement path and compare decisions. ARB does this at small scale already: OPA against Cedar (D22), in-process against framework hosts behind gateways (D51, D53), direct against FastMCP gateways (D32).

And add one shared checkpoint in front of the systems that matter most (payments, customer records, HR): your gateway and PDP with trusted facts, which every platform's agents must pass. It is the one enforcement point you fully control, and it makes the platform mix matter less for the highest-risk effects.

45.4The registry of record

The second prediction question: none of them by default. Choose a registry of record explicitly and reconcile the others into it:

OptionStrengthWeakness
The dominant platform's registry (for example Agent 365 in a Microsoft-heavy estate)Native identity and lifecycle integrationOther platforms' agents are second-class
An IT service and risk platform (ServiceNow AI Control Tower, IBM)Risk workflows, approvals, CMDB linkageEnforcement still elsewhere
Your own (as in ARB's CTL-0023)Exactly your schema; feeds your gate and enforcementYou build and run it

Whichever you choose, reconcile on a schedule by resolved subject, not by display name, and treat orphans, maker-credential agents and unauthenticated agents as findings (D59).

45.5Policy portability

Generic Each platform speaks a different policy language: Cedar and Dogwood (AWS), CEL in IAM access policies (Google), API Management policy expressions (Microsoft), ACS policy YAML (Microsoft's open specification), Rego or Cedar in your PDP. The first prediction question's answer is the same as within your build: author the rule once, in your catalog's terms, translate it per platform, and keep a parity test that runs the same cases against every translation. ARB's OPA/Cedar parity suite (every golden refund, boundary case, read and fixture order) and its AGT parity spike (D63, which found rules AGT could not express) are the template. A translation that cannot pass parity is a finding, not a deployment.

45.6Evidence normalisation

  • One decision schema: subject, agent, version digest, action, inputs (minimised), facts read, rule and control ids, decision, enforcement point, platform, timestamp, provenance.
  • Provenance labels: your chained and signed records versus imported platform logs; the second kind is useful but weaker, and reports should say which is which.
  • Traces in OpenTelemetry with the GenAI semantic conventions, joined to decisions by run and step ids (Chapter 31).
  • Catalog ids namespaced by catalog, so two builds' CTL-0020 cannot be confused (finding F-02).

45.7Identity across platforms

Each platform issues its own agent identities (SPIFFE-based Agent Identity, Entra Agent ID, AgentCore Identity). Cross-platform calls need federation: workload identity federation between clouds, OAuth on-behalf-of flows for user delegation, and signed A2A agent cards between agents. Above them, your directory resolves every identity to one subject, as ARB's D33 resolves many spellings to one subject; that resolved subject is what the registry, the PDP and the evidence use (Chapter 18).

45.8How portable to be

The third prediction question: build on one platform, govern across all. A GCP-standard AICoE gains most by building its own agents on GEAP well, and keeping three things portable because they are its own: the rulebook and catalog, the evidence and registry, and the enforcement logic behind seams and gateways. Full portability of every agent costs a lot and is rarely exercised. ARB's design shows the cheap version that pays: seams and an import firewall, gateways that any framework can reach, and decision-equivalence tests, so that moving is a measured change rather than a rewrite (Chapter 7).

45.9Keeping the map current

The capability map above was true for about a quarter. Treat it as a living document: re-verify it quarterly against release notes (this book's register lists it), and adopt a native feature only when it meets the control's contract and passes the control's falsifiers on that platform (Chapter 24). A native feature that is easier but weaker is a decision to record, not a default.

45.10In the room

In the room
  • "We're on three clouds and two SaaS platforms. Can one product govern all our agents?" "Discovery and inventory, nearly. Enforcement, no. Keep one rulebook, one registry of record and one evidence store; enforce through each platform's native points plus your own checkpoint in front of the systems that matter; and test that the platforms decide alike."
  • "Which platform governs agents best?" "It depends on the job: Microsoft for estate identity and data protection, AWS for deterministic tool policy, Google for agent identity design and the model path. None provides tamper-evident decision evidence or configuration-bound sign-off; those are yours everywhere."
