PART III · CHAPTER 11

What governance must do when software acts

An AI system that answers can be governed mostly before it ships. An agent that acts has to be governed at the moment it acts, every time, and the evidence of that has to outlive the act. This chapter sets out what changes, what does not, and the vocabulary the rest of the book uses.

11.1From outputs to acts

Generic A model that is wrong produces a wrong sentence. A person reads it, and the person is the control: they decide whether to act on it. An agent that is wrong produces a wrong act: it refunds the wrong customer, closes the wrong ticket, emails a contract to the wrong party, grants access it should not. There may be no person between the decision and the effect. That one shift changes five properties of the risk, and each one moves where governance has to work.

PropertyAnswering systemActing systemWhat it does to governance
ReversibilityA bad answer can be ignoredSome acts cannot be undone (a payment, a sent message, a deleted record)Controls must sit before the effect, not in a review afterwards
Speed and volumePaced by a human readerMachine speed, in loops, many calls per taskPer-action human review does not scale; automation must decide most calls, people only the consequential ones
AuthorityHas none; borrows the reader'sHolds credentials and calls systems of recordIdentity, least privilege and delegation become governance concerns, not just IT ones
Attack surfaceIts input is the user's promptReads documents, tool results, other agents' messages, any of which can carry instructionsUntrusted content must be kept from steering acts; the attacker no longer needs to be the user
CompositionOne model, one answerAgents call tools and other agents; authority passes along chainsWho authorised the final act must be answerable across hops

Put together, these mean that the defining activity of agent governance is run-time enforcement with evidence. Design reviews, risk assessments and pre-release testing still matter, but they decide what the run-time mechanism must enforce; they cannot replace it. Most of this book is about that mechanism and the system around it.

11.2What carries over from AI governance

Generic The good news for any organisation with an AI governance programme is that most of its management system carries over. The question for each component is whether it holds unchanged, needs extending, or needs replacing.

ComponentVerdict for agentsWhy
Policy, principles, prohibited usesExtendPolicies written for content and data rarely say what kinds of action are allowed, how much autonomy can be granted, or who may grant more
Accountability and committeesHoldsThe same risk committee can own agent risk; it needs new decision rights (autonomy grants, CRITICAL sign-off)
InventoryExtendA register of models and use cases becomes a registry of agents as actors: owner, identity, tools, data, tier, edges, status
Risk and impact assessmentExtendThe scoring must include what the agent can change, how independently and how far a mistake spreads (Chapter 13)
Data governanceExtendAgents move data across boundaries at run time and keep it in memory (Chapter 21)
Model validation and testingExtendAdd trajectory and tool-use evaluation, attack testing, and proof that each control blocks (Chapter 23, Chapter 24)
Transparency to usersHoldsDisclosure that AI is involved; plus disclosure of what it may do on the user's behalf
SecurityExtendPrompt injection, tool poisoning, confused-deputy and excessive agency are new threat classes (Chapter 32)
Monitoring and incident managementExtendMonitor actions and refusals, not only quality; add a kill switch and action-level forensics (Chapter 31)
Design-time review as the main controlReplaceIt cannot see what the agent will be asked, what it will read, or what it will decide; it can only decide what the run-time controls must be

11.3What is genuinely new

Generic Seven concerns are new or much harder with agents. Each has a mechanism in Part IV and an example in your builds.

ConcernThe failureThe mechanismIn the book
Identity and authorityThe agent runs on its builder's credentials, so every user borrows the builder's permissions; or the caller's identity is a string anyone can typeA resolved identity per agent; capabilities signed, not asserted; on-behalf-of where the user's rights should bound the actChapter 18
Acts, not outputsHarm arrives through a tool call, sometimes irreversibleA policy decision on facts from trusted state before every consequential callChapter 16
Oversight at scale"A person reviews it" turns into rubber-stamping under volumeApproval only where the stakes are high, bound to one exact action, by someone other than the requester, with friction and fatigue signalsChapter 19
Content that steersA document, ticket comment or tool result says "ignore your instructions and…"Untrusted content enters as data, never as instruction; acts are authorised by policy, not by what the model was persuaded ofChapter 20
Chains of agentsA asks B, B asks C; nobody can say who authorised the final actDeclared edges, resolved senders, messages without authority, provenance across the hopChapter 5
Change after approvalA model alias moves, a prompt is edited, a tool is added, memory accumulates; the approved agent is no longer the running agentPinned models, derived tiers, sign-off bound to a configuration digest, re-evaluation on changeChapter 15
SprawlLow-code tools make agents cheap; estates of hundreds appear before anyone counts themDiscovery plus a registry as a run-time precondition: no registry entry, no productionChapter 12, Chapter 45

11.4A census of one agent

Your build The argument above can be made from first principles, but the strongest version of it is empirical. ARB's first phase (P1) built a competent, ungoverned customer-service agent on Google ADK: a BM25 knowledge-base retriever, read tools for orders and customers, and one write, issue_refund, wired to a payments adapter. It ran live on a local model. Then the build wrote down every way it could cause harm, each with the file and line where the gap lived and a test that demonstrated it. The result is docs/governance/CONTROL_GAPS.md: fifteen gaps in an agent with four tools.

FamilyGapsWhat they have in common
Correctness of the actGAP-01 unbounded amount · 02 refund above order total · 03 refund on a non-refundable order · 12 currency silently assumed · 13 negative or zero amountsThe tool trusted its arguments. Every one is a precondition a policy can check on facts the system already holds.
Who may actGAP-04 no approval · 05 no separation of duties · 08 unauthenticated callerThe system believed a claim because it was made (D20's diagnosis).
Volume and repetitionGAP-06 no idempotency · 07 no rate or value budgetA loop or a retry multiplies one mistake.
What the model reads and saysGAP-14 no injection boundary · 15 citations never verifiedThe model's inputs and outputs were trusted as if they were the system's own.
ProofGAP-09 PII in evidence · 10 no output contract on the write · 11 evidence not tamper-evidentEven where the act was right, nothing could prove it, or the proof itself leaked.

Two of these were found live rather than by reading. GAP-15 surfaced when the local model answered a policy question with a citation, [KB-0000], to an article that does not exist. And the P1 run that motivates Chapter 16 showed the agent call get_order, receive the order, and refund an amount the order did not support. The model performed the check and ignored it.

Fifteen gaps in four tools is the number to remember when someone says their agents are "low risk because the team is good". The gaps are not a sign of bad engineering; they are what any capable agent has before governance is added. The list kept growing as the build did: cross-agent edges added four (GAP-21 to 24), the evolution phases added five more (GAP-25 to 29: sign-off not bound to configuration, unbounded model consumption, unpinned models, no governed inventory, unverified MCP tools). Each became a control. That traceability, every control to a measured gap, is the discipline Chapter 14 builds on.

11.5Vocabulary at engineering resolution

Clients, vendors and analysts use these words loosely and often interchangeably. The book uses them precisely. The right-hand column is the test that tells you which one someone actually means.

TermIsIs notThe test
ModelThe network that maps tokens to tokensAn agent; it has no tools, memory or loop of its ownCould you swap it for another provider's without changing behaviour you govern? Then the behaviour lives elsewhere
HarnessThe software that turns a model into an agent: loop, instructions, tools, context, memory, error handling, extension pointsGovernance, even when it has hooks for itIt decides how the agent works; it does not decide what the agent may do
Platform / runtimeWhere agents are built, hosted and run (Agent Engine, Copilot Studio, AgentCore, your containers)The rulebookIt supplies enforcement points; someone still has to configure what they enforce
GuardrailAn automatic check on content going into or out of a modelAn authorisation decision about an actDoes it look at text or at a proposed act with its facts? Text: guardrail
Policy decision (PDP)A deterministic decision on whether a specific act is allowed, from factsA prompt instructionWould the same input always get the same answer, regardless of phrasing?
ApprovalA signed human decision bound to one specific actA human "in the loop" somewhere in the processCan it be replayed for a different act? Then it is not an approval
EvalA structured measurement of behaviour against a dataset and a rubricA proof that a control worksDoes it measure quality, or does it show a mechanism refusing? The latter is conformance
Conformance testA test that drives the real enforcement plane and shows a control fireA unit test of the rule in isolationWould disabling the control make it fail?
ObservabilityRecording what the system did so it can be monitored and debuggedAuditIs it optimised for an engineer at 2 a.m. or an auditor in six months?
EvidenceA tamper-evident, minimised record that a decision was made and an act happened, attributable to a controlLogsCan a fresh process verify it was not altered?
ControlA mechanism with an objective, a trigger, an owner, evidence, a test and an exception pathA policy statementCan you point to the line of code that refuses, and the test that proves it?
GovernanceThe system that sets the rules, assigns accountability, decides which controls each agent needs, checks the evidence before and after release, and keeps it true over timeAny one of the items aboveIt uses all of them and replaces none

Two further terms matter for the rest of the book. A global invariant is a control that holds for every agent at every tier (deny by default, no action without evidence, executor never approver). A posture is the state of a control on the ladder in the figure above: unguarded, specified, enforced, verified. When someone says an agent "has" a control, ask which rung they mean.

11.6Where governance acts

Generic AI governance concentrates its work in two places: before release (assessment, validation, approval) and after release (monitoring). Agent governance keeps both and adds a third that carries most of the weight: the run-time path of every consequential act. The figure shows where each sits along one act.

The middle box is not a replacement for the outer two; it is what they configure and what they verify. Pre-release work decides which controls this agent's path must carry. Post-act work checks, from evidence, that the path behaved, and turns any change into a new pre-release decision. That loop (author, enforce, prove, revise) is the reference model of Chapter 12.

11.7The regulatory posture: self-governance with evidence

Generic As of late 2026 no major regime regulates "agents" as a category. Regimes regulate uses and their effects: the EU AI Act classifies systems by use and imposes obligations on high-risk uses, transparency duties and general-purpose model duties; data protection law governs personal data and automated decisions; consumer protection, anti-discrimination, financial-services and health regulation govern what the act does in their domain. Everything an agent does is therefore already regulated, through what it does rather than how it is built.

The practical consequence is that regulators, auditors and boards expect firms to govern their agents themselves and to be able to show it: which agents exist, who owns each, what each may do, what stops it doing more, and what happened when it acted. That is a demand for evidence of operating controls, which is exactly what the enforcement and evidence planes produce. A programme that can only show policies and committee minutes will struggle; one that can show a conformance report and a verifiable action trail will not.

Check before quoting

Application dates of the EU AI Act's obligations, the status of any national or sector guidance on agentic AI (Singapore's agentic AI framework, financial supervisors' model-risk guidance, health regulators' guidance on AI-enabled decisions) and any 2026 amendments change often. Chapter 48 covers each with dates; confirm against the primary text before putting a date or article number in writing.

11.9In the room

In the room
  • "Our AI policy already covers agents." Ask three questions: does it say which kinds of action an agent may take, who may grant an agent more autonomy, and whether an agent may run on a person's credentials? If any answer is "it doesn't say", it covers content, not acts.
  • "How many agents do you have?" Ask how they know. A count from a registry that agents must be in to run is a fact; a count from a survey is an estimate, usually low.
  • "Governance will slow us down." The census answer: a carefully built agent with four tools had fifteen distinct ways to cause harm. Governance is how such an agent gets through a security review at all; a fast lane keeps the low-risk majority moving.

11.10Prove it

Defend cold · pairs with R01 and GF-2
  1. RecallName the five properties that change between an answering and an acting system, and the seven new concerns.
  2. ApplyPick five of the fifteen P1 gaps and name, for each, the concern it belongs to and the control family that closed it.
  3. ApplyA colleague calls Model Armor "our agent governance". Using the vocabulary table, say precisely what it is and what it is not.
  4. DesignA bank's AI governance programme has a model inventory, a validation team and a quarterly committee. Write the five changes it needs before its first agent with a write tool goes live.
  5. Trade-offArgue for and against putting a human approval on every agent action in the first year.
