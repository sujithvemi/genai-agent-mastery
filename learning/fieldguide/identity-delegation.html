PART IV · CHAPTER 18

Identity, delegation and capability

Every act an agent takes is taken under some authority. Governance has to know whose, bound it to what the act needs, and be able to prove it afterwards, which means identities that are resolved rather than spelled, capabilities that are signed rather than asserted, and keys held so that the parties who verify cannot forge.

18.1The original error: believing a claim

Your build Three of the fifteen gaps in ARB's ungoverned agent were about authority: no approval (GAP-04), no separation of duties (GAP-05), and an unauthenticated caller whose principal was whatever string it passed (GAP-08). D20 named what they share: the system believed a claim because it was made. A header saying hitl_approved: true is the caller approving itself. The fix is the same for all three: a claim is worth nothing unless it carries a signature the enforcement plane can check for itself, on every call, against its own scope.

The rest of the chapter is that sentence worked out: who the agent is, whose authority it acts on, how capabilities and approvals are represented, how identities are compared, and how keys are held.

18.2Who the agent is

Generic An agent needs its own identity, distinct from the person who built it and from the users who call it. The most common failure in low-code estates is the opposite: an agent published with its builder's connection credentials, so every user of the agent silently exercises the builder's permissions. The second most common is a shared service account across many agents, so no action can be attributed and no agent can be revoked alone.

Identity formWhat it givesWhere
Workload identity (service account, managed identity)A non-human principal the cloud's IAM can grant toEvery cloud; GCP service accounts
Keyless federation (Workload Identity Federation, OIDC to cloud)Short-lived credentials exchanged from an external identity, no stored keysCI pipelines, cross-cloud workloads; your M0 work
SPIFFE / SPIRECryptographic workload identity and mTLS between services, platform-neutralService meshes, multi-cloud estates
Agent-specific identity VerifyA directory object per agent with lifecycle, owner and access reviews (for example Microsoft Entra Agent ID, Google's agent identity in its agent platform)Vendor agent platforms
A resolved directory subjectAn immutable id, a kind (human or service), active status and aliases, answered by a directory the plane trustsARB's platform/identity seam (D33)

Whatever the form, the governance requirements are the same: one identity per agent, an accountable owner on it, least privilege on what it can reach, credentials that are short-lived or never leave a key service, and a revocation path that has been tested.

18.3On whose authority

Generic An agent acting for a user can use one of three authorities, and the choice is a design decision with security consequences.

  • The agent's own authority. Simple, and right when the agent operates on data and systems no individual user's rights should govern (a reconciliation job). Wrong when users with different rights call the same agent: the agent becomes a confused deputy (Hardy, 1988), a program with more authority than its caller, tricked into using it on the caller's behalf. An HR assistant reading all HR documents with its own permissions will answer one employee's question with another's data.
  • On behalf of the user. The agent exchanges the user's token for a narrower one (OAuth 2.0 Token Exchange, RFC 8693), carrying both identities: the user as subject, the agent as actor. Effective permission is the intersection: the user's rights, narrowed to the agent's purpose. Every act is attributable to both.
  • Hybrid. Reads are bounded by the user's rights (permission-aware retrieval, Chapter 21); acts are bounded by the agent's own signed capabilities plus policy and, where required, a human approval. This is the right default for most enterprise agents, and it is roughly ARB's shape: the customer-service agent acts under its own capability, and the refund is bounded by policy facts and an approval, not by the customer's rights.

18.4Capability tokens

Your build enforcement/tokens.py defines two signed token types with one mechanism. A CapabilityToken says who is calling and what they may do: it is minted by an IdentityService for a principal and a scoped set of tool names, with a nonce and an expiry (15 minutes by default, token_ttl_seconds: 900 in params). The PEP verifies it on every call; an unsigned, expired or tampered token makes the principal unverified, and GBL-DENY-01 takes it from there.

The same token does a second job that is easy to miss: since P4-S1 it gates reads of the evidence detail store, where the free text removed from the open log is kept (CTL-0009). "Who may read the sensitive half of the audit trail" is answered by the same verified-claim mechanism as "who may call this tool", not by a second, weaker one. One mechanism for authority, reused, is easier to reason about than several.

pm-agent pm-agent applied the capability idea one level down, to its own architecture. A review found that its tool registry's invoke method was public, so code holding the chokepoint could run a tool around all four of its collaborators with no evidence event. The fix (D-PM-P3-b) made off-PEP execution impossible by construction: the registry mints a secret capability when it is created and hands it to exactly one chokepoint through a one-shot bind(); invoke requires it. This is the object-capability pattern: authority is a reference you hold, not a check someone remembers to make.

18.5Approval tokens

Your build An ApprovalToken says that a named human approved this exact write. It is signed over the order, the amount in minor units, the currency, the approver, a nonce and an expiry, and verified against the call it is presented for. The red team (P5-S3) found three ways a valid signature was worth more than it said, and each fix generalises:

18.6Resolve, don't compare

Your build D33 is the most important identity decision in the build, and its reasoning transfers to any system. After RT-08, separation of duties compared two identifiers after folding case, whitespace, format characters and compatibility forms. The red team then used a Cyrillic letter that looks like a Latin one: it folds to itself, so the respelled agent name passed as a different person. A confusables table would win that round and lose the next.

D33 changed the primitive from comparison to resolution. A directory seam (DirectoryService.resolve(identifier) → Subject | None) answers with an immutable subject_id, a primary id, a kind (human or service), an active flag and aliases. The approval service resolves both the approver and the executor, and separation of duties compares two subject ids. The result is falsifiable in both directions:

  • A homoglyph spelling resolves to nobody, so it is refused as not a person (CTL-0004 answers, because it is not the executor; it is no one).
  • The agent's own service-account email address, which shares no prefix with its name, resolves to the executor's subject and is refused as self-approval. No string normalisation could ever have caught that.

Two smaller rules make the seam trustworthy. "Not found" (None) is a positive answer; a directory that cannot be reached raises DirectoryUnavailableError, so an outage is never recorded as a forged identity. And since D40, an approver must be a subject of kind human: a service subject can never approve or satisfy separation of duties, which closes agent-approves-agent by construction. The old fold survives only as a second check that can add a refusal, never remove one.

Gotcha

Normalisation is still needed; it just is not the decision. A directory keys its index on normalised identifiers. The mistake is letting a normalised string be the identity. When a client says their approval workflow checks "approver ≠ requester", ask what it compares.

18.7Signing and trust domains

Your build ARB's signing went through three stages, and the reasons for each are the design lesson.

StageMechanismRight whenLimit
P3 (D20)HMAC-SHA256 over canonical JSON, per-process random keyOne trust domain: the approval service and the PEP are the same processWhoever can verify can also forge; a fresh process cannot verify at all
E4-S3Ed25519: a Signer holds the private key; a Verifier holds only the public key and has no sign methodVerifiers in other trust domains: auditors, gateways, peer agentsKey custody is now the problem
E4-S3Cloud KMS EC_SIGN_ED25519: the private key never leaves KMS; the runner holds signerVerifier on that key onlyProduction: non-repudiation for a key that never leaves its holderA network call per signature; KMS availability is on the path

Two details of the KMS binding are worth copying. Every signature KMS returns is verified locally against the public key before it is handed out, so a corrupted, substituted or truncated response becomes an error, never a signature. And one algorithm everywhere means one verify path serves both the local and the KMS binding.

D28 records the reasoning that made the move necessary, and it is one of the most transferable sentences in the build. Before asymmetric keys, the audit CLI did not check signatures at all, and said so, because a per-process HMAC key cannot be shared with a later process: "claiming to check signatures here, against a key generated fresh in the same call, verifying nothing about the original writer, would have been worse than not checking them." In short: don't build a check that cannot fail honestly.

18.8Identity across agents

Your build When agents talk to each other, the sender of a message is another identity claim. GAP-22 was exactly that: from: knowledge_advisor was an unresolved string, and being registered was treated as being authorised. CTL-0017 requires the sender to be a resolved subject (through the same directory, kind service) and the channel to be authorised by the declared topology, enforced in Rego, not by a new decision point. The PIP keeps the sender's own statement under a field named sender_asserted, and no rule decides on it. For out-of-process hosts, the gateways authenticate the host channel with signed requests from a resolved, credentialed service subject (gateways/channel.py). Chapter 5 covers the protocol side.

18.9Identity lifecycle for agents

Generic Joiner-mover-leaver processes exist for people; agents need the same, and most organisations have nothing.

  • Joiner: identity created at intake (G0), owner recorded, no permissions until the design gate grants the minimum.
  • Mover: a new tool or data source is a permission change, reviewed like any other, and a re-tiering trigger.
  • Leaver: retirement revokes the identity, its capabilities and keys, and removes it from every peer's declared edges.
  • Review: agents included in access reviews, with the owner attesting; an agent whose owner has left is suspended, not inherited silently.
  • Keys: rotation on a schedule and on compromise, with old public keys retained for as long as evidence they signed must remain verifiable.

18.12In the room

In the room
  • "Whose credentials do your agents run on?" Usually the most revealing question in a discovery session. Ask for the count of agents with personal connections.
  • "How do you know the approver isn't the agent?" "The approver and the executor are both resolved in the directory to immutable subjects, approvers must be human subjects, and the approval is signed over the exact act. An alias of the agent's own service account is caught as self-approval."
  • "Can your auditors check the signatures?" "With public keys only. The private keys live in KMS and the verifiers cannot sign, so a verified record is non-repudiable."
