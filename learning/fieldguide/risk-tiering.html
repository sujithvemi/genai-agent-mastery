PART III · CHAPTER 13

Fleet-first risk tiering

How one rulebook decides, mechanically and repeatably, which controls each agent in a fleet must carry, and why the rulebook has to be written for the fleet rather than for the first agent you govern.

13.1Why tier at all

Generic A governance programme that treats every agent the same fails in both directions. Apply critical-grade controls to a read-only FAQ bot and builders route around you; the shadow agents that result are ungoverned by construction. Apply FAQ-grade controls to an agent that moves money and the first incident sets the programme back a year. Proportionality is the mechanism that lets a small central team govern hundreds of agents: the agent's risk decides the controls, and the controls decide the path to production.

Most governance programmes say they are "risk-based". Few can show the function that turns an agent into a control list. This chapter is that function, and three properties of it matter more than any particular scoring scheme:

  • Deterministic. Two assessors scoring the same agent honestly reach the same tier. A tier that depends on who is in the room is a negotiation.
  • Monotone. Raising any input can never lower the result. If giving an agent more autonomy could ever reduce its control set, the scheme has a hole an attacker or a hurried builder will find.
  • Generative. The tier does not produce advice; it produces a list of required controls that a gate can check. Tiering that ends in a PDF is not tiering.

13.2The five axes

Your build ARB scores every agent, and each of its tools, on five ordinal axes held in governance/taxonomy/axes.yaml. Values are listed low to high and the position of a value is its score. The file says so in capitals: do not reorder, because the ordinal position is the score.

AxisValues, low → highThe question it answers
data_sensitivitynone · internal · PII · sensitive/regulatedHow bad is it if what the agent sees leaks?
action_classread-only · reversible-write · irreversible/financial-writeCan it change the world, and can the change be undone?
exposureinternal-only · authenticated-external · publicWho can reach it, and therefore who can attack it?
autonomysuggests · acts-with-confirmation · acts-autonomouslyIs a person between the decision and the effect?
blast_radiussingle-record · multi-record · systemicHow far does one mistake spread?

Generic Notice what the axes are not. None of them scores the model ("is it GPT or Gemini?") or the use case's business value. Model choice is a control concern (pinning, evaluation, provenance), and value is a portfolio concern. The axes describe the agent's capability to cause harm, which is the thing governance is accountable for. Each axis also maps to a family of controls you will meet later: sensitivity to data controls, action class to write controls, exposure to screening and content safety, autonomy to oversight, blast radius to limits and budgets. That correspondence is why five is enough.

Two scoring rules keep the axes honest. Score the agent at its worst reachable case, not its typical one: a support agent that usually answers questions but can issue a refund is a financial-write agent. And score from the agent's real tool surface, not its prompt: if the tool exists and the agent's identity can call it, the capability is there whatever the instructions say.

13.3Banding: scores to a tier

Your build governance/taxonomy/tiers.yaml turns the five scores into one of four tiers with an ordered, first-match rule. The tiering engine (src/aicoe/governance/tiering/engine.py, function derive_tier) implements it exactly.

Read the ladder as three design decisions, each of which you should be able to defend.

Highest-wins, not additive

Generic An additive or weighted score lets a low value on one axis compensate for a high value on another. "It moves money autonomously, but it is internal-only and touches one record, so it averages out to MEDIUM" is exactly the reasoning that produces incidents. Highest-wins says the agent is as risky as its most dangerous capability. That is conservative by design, and it is the right default for a control system: false positives cost a heavier gate; false negatives cost an unrecoverable action.

A pair clause for CRITICAL

Your build CRITICAL is reached by an OR of two clauses. The first is a specific combination: action_class = irreversible/financial-write and autonomy = acts-autonomously. Neither alone is catastrophic (an irreversible write a human confirms, or an autonomous agent that only reads), but together they turn one wrong decision into an unrecoverable loss with no human in the loop. The second clause is breadth: two or more axes at their top value.

Look closely and you will find something the build never says out loud. With today's value lists, both values in the pair clause are the top values of their axes, so any agent that satisfies the pair also satisfies "two or more at top". The pair clause is logically subsumed. What it still contributes is the reason string: the evidence says the agent is CRITICAL because it moves money autonomously, in business terms, instead of "two axes at top". The comment in tiers.yaml says the pair matters "even if no single axis is at absolute top", which is not true with the current lists. Assumption It was probably written when the action-class scale had a value above financial-write, or before the second clause existed. Take it to R04 as a question, not a bug: if a fourth action class is ever added (say, security-or-identity-change), the pair clause becomes load-bearing again.

Ordinal, with no "mid" ambiguity

Each test asks a yes/no question about positions. There is no "moderately sensitive" judgement inside the engine; the judgement happens once, when a person scores an axis, and it is recorded with a rationale in the agent's manifest. That separation is what makes the derivation reproducible: arguments about an agent become arguments about one axis value, which is a much smaller disagreement.

Gotcha

A tier is saturating. Once an agent is CRITICAL, raising another axis changes nothing about the tier. Whether it changes the control set depends on whether some control's applies_when reads that axis. Moving customer_service_ops from authenticated-external to public changes neither its tier nor its controls today, because the only exposure-scoped control (CTL-0025, content safety) already applies from authenticated-external. If you think a public agent needs more, that is a catalog decision, not a tiering one.

13.4CRITICAL changes treatment, not just count

Your build A common mistake is to treat the tier as a volume knob: more risk, more controls. In ARB the step to CRITICAL changes how controls apply. tiers.yaml carries four treatment flags the gate enforces:

FlagWhat it means at CRITICAL
waiver_forbiddenThe non_waivable set (CTL-0004 approval on writes, CTL-0005 separation of duties, GBL-SOD-01) cannot be waived, by anyone.
approval_mandatoryHuman approval is mandatory and must be signed.
realtime_oversightOversight is live, not a periodic review.
exec_signoffDeployment needs executive sign-off, bound to a configuration digest (CTL-0022, the only control with min_tier: CRITICAL).

The point is that the same control can exist at HIGH with a waiver path and at CRITICAL without one. A HIGH agent whose approval step is temporarily waived during an outage is a managed risk; a CRITICAL agent whose approval step is waived is a different system from the one the risk committee accepted.

13.5The derivation rule

Your build This is the load-bearing sentence of the whole framework, from decision D14:

required(control) ⇔ min_tier(control) ≤ tier(agent)
                     AND applies_when(control) holds for the agent
required(GBL-*)   ⇔ always

Two fields on the control do the work, and both are properties of the control, never of any agent:

  • min_tier is the lowest tier at which the control's risk is present. Rate and value budgets matter from MEDIUM (CTL-0007); approval on writes matters from HIGH (CTL-0004); inventory completeness matters for everyone (CTL-0023, LOW).
  • applies_when is an optional predicate that scopes the control to a risk shape. CTL-0001, the refund ceiling, applies only when action_class = irreversible/financial-write; evidence minimisation (CTL-0009) only when data_sensitivity ≥ PII. A control without a predicate applies to every agent at or above its min_tier.

The three global invariants (GBL-DENY-01 deny by default, GBL-EVID-01 no action without evidence, GBL-SOD-01 executor is never the approver) are required for every agent regardless of tier. They are the floor the rest of the catalog stands on.

The engine function required_control_ids is a single list comprehension. That brevity is the design: the intelligence lives in authored YAML a governance lead can read, not in code only an engineer can.

Why D14 exists: the agent-first anti-pattern

Your build While ARB was being built, the implementing model proposed setting min_tier: HIGH on all fifteen controls of the time, so that the CRITICAL customer-service agent would pull the whole set. It would have worked for that agent and been wrong for every other agent the framework would ever meet: a MEDIUM read-only assistant would have pulled nothing, because every control would have sat above its tier. You caught it and named the rule: fleet-first, not agent-first. Author each control's min_tier from the control's own risk, then let any particular agent's set fall out. The test that guards it, in tests/test_governance_tiering.py, asserts the control set of a hypothetical MEDIUM read-only PII agent (the STAR_SET), so that fitting the catalog to one agent breaks the build.

13.7A fleet is a graph

Your build When the two agents were wired together (P7-S3), a natural instinct was to compose them: treat the pair as one super-agent whose tier is the maximum of the two. D40 rejected it, and the reasoning is worth owning because clients will propose the same thing.

The argument has three steps.

  1. Max-over-axes derives nothing. The composed agent's axes would just be the CRITICAL agent's axes. You learn nothing new about the risk of the interaction itself.
  2. Re-scoring upstream makes the fleet unstable. If a consumer can raise its supplier's tier, onboarding any new consumer reopens the supplier's gate. In a fleet of hundreds of agents that is a governance outage every week.
  3. The new risk is on the edge, so govern the edge. An inbound message from another agent can carry an injected instruction; a sender can be spoofed; an undeclared edge can appear; influence can go unrecorded. Those four risks became CTL-0016…CTL-0019, all at min_tier: LOW with an interaction predicate. They attach to any agent of that shape, of any tier.

D40 adds the principle that ties the chapter to the rest of the book: a message has no authority. A cross-agent message is data, never an instruction or a permission. That is the same rule as "an instruction is not a control", extended from the prompt to the agent boundary. You will see it again in Chapter 20 (injection) and Chapter 18 (delegation).

13.8Fleet-first, one layer down

Your build Fleet-first at derivation is necessary but not sufficient. When the advisor onboarded, its derived set was right, but four of its required controls could not fire. The Rego bindings for CTL-0007, CTL-0010, CTL-0013 and CTL-0014 had been written behind an is_write guard, because the first agent's only risk was its write. The catalog said the controls applied; the enforcement could not reach them.

D39 ruled this a binding gap, not a catalog error, and wrote the standing principle: an enforcement binding is authored to the control's trigger, not to the first agent's shape. It is the D14 anti-pattern one layer down. The fix grew each control a read-path half (validate the query, validate the answer contract, refuse a poisoned document steering the answer, rate-limit reads) instead of forking the catalog into read and write variants.

Two riders came with it, and both are good general rules:

  • Conformance must be positive per agent. For each required control, show the binding firing for this agent. A control required but unreachable is present, not enforced.
  • Vacuous satisfaction must be explicit, and its precondition tested. GBL-SOD-01 holds for the advisor because it has no write to approve. The conformance check records "not applicable, satisfied by shape" and asserts that no approval-requiring write exists, so the day someone adds one, the vacuity assertion fails and forces a re-derivation instead of letting a dormant invariant silently become load-bearing.

13.9Re-tiering on change

Generic A tier is a function of the agent's current capabilities, so any change to capabilities is a risk event. The changes that should trigger re-derivation are exactly the inputs to the function: a new or changed tool (action class, data, blast radius), a new channel or audience (exposure), removing a confirmation step (autonomy), a new peer or MCP server (shape). Changes to the model or instructions do not move the tier, but they do invalidate evidence that the controls work, so they re-enter the lifecycle at the evaluation gate (Chapter 15).

Your build ARB makes this mechanical in two ways. The tier is derived from the manifest on every gate run, never stored as an attribute someone could forget to update. At CRITICAL, sign-off is bound to a configuration digest (CTL-0022): change the manifest, model pin or policy bundle and the digest changes, so the old sign-off no longer matches and the gate refuses. Re-pinning customer_service_ops from a local model to Vertex in E4 went through exactly this re-gate (D66).

Waiting on the builds

The two-agent table is the chapter's evidence that the catalog is fleet-first. With only two archetypes, it shows that sets differ; it cannot show that the axes are sufficient. When a third archetype lands (for example a reversible-write agent at HIGH, or an agent scored on sensitive/regulated data), re-run derive_for_manifest for all three, extend the table, and check whether any new risk needed a new applies_when predicate rather than a new axis.

Waiting on the builds

If the action scale gains a value above financial writes (identity or security changes are the usual candidate), the CRITICAL pair clause stops being subsumed by the two-at-top clause. Revisit 13.3 and the R04 question.

Score gamingA builder scores autonomy as "acts-with-confirmation" because a confirmation dialog exists that users click through.Score from the tool surface and identity, not the UI; second-assessor review at HIGH and above; the axis rationale lives in the manifest and is reviewed at the gate.

13.12Client translation

In the room

The one-minute version for a client: every agent is registered and scored on five things (how sensitive its data is, whether it can change things, who can reach it, how independently it acts, how far a mistake spreads); the score sets a tier; the tier and the agent's shape decide its controls automatically; a change re-scores it. Three follow-ups come next, and you now have the answers:

  • "Why not our existing risk matrix?" Because a matrix lets likelihood offset impact, and for an agent the dangerous capability is present whether or not it is likely to be used. Highest-wins keeps the tier tied to what the agent can do.
  • "Won't this explode into a control list per agent?" No. Each control declares its own lowest tier and the shape it applies to, once. Agents' lists are derived. Onboarding a new kind of agent is a configuration change, which ARB proves with a second archetype and zero framework edits.
  • "What happens when agents call each other?" Each agent keeps its own tier; the connection is governed as its own object, with controls that treat every message from another agent as data with no authority.
Check before quoting

Regulatory risk classes are not operational tiers. The EU AI Act's categories (prohibited practices, high-risk systems under Article 6 and its annexes, transparency obligations, minimal risk) are legal classifications of a system's use; your tiers classify an agent's capability. A high-risk system under the Act will usually land HIGH or CRITICAL, but the mapping runs through the crosswalk (Chapter 48), not through the tiering engine. Confirm article references against the current consolidated text before putting them in writing.
