A reference model: planes, layers, enforcement points

A map you can assess an estate against, design an architecture from, assign owners on, and sequence a roadmap with. Three planes say where the work happens; ten layers say what capability is needed; four kinds of enforcement point say where a control can bite.

12.1Why a reference model

Generic A reference model is useful when it lets different people answer different questions from one picture. An executive asks "where are we weak?"; an architect asks "where does this control run?"; a programme lead asks "what do we build first?"; an auditor asks "who owns this, and where is the proof?". A model that answers only one of these becomes shelfware. The one in this chapter is built to answer all four, and it is grounded in the way your reference build is actually laid out, so each box has a directory you can open.

12.2Three planes and a loop

Generic Every governance mechanism lives in one of three planes, and the three form a loop.

  • The authoring plane says what must be true: taxonomy, tiering rules, the control catalog, policy source, parameters, the registry of agents and edges, sign-offs and waivers. It is written by people, reviewed, versioned and ratified.
  • The enforcement plane makes it true at the moment of the act: the design-time gate that refuses a release, and the run-time enforcement points that refuse a call. It is code, configured by the authoring plane.
  • The assurance plane proves it was true: the evidence each decision and act leaves, the conformance runs that show controls fire, evaluations, red-team results, and audit. Its findings flow back into the authoring plane as changes.

Your build ARB's ADR-0001 put this split into the repository layout from the start: authored governance semantics at the root governance/ (written by you), governance code under src/aicoe/governance/ and src/aicoe/enforcement/ (written by the build), evidence and assurance under evidence/, evals/, tests/ and the audit CLI. The rule that the build may not author semantics (CHARTER, "fleet-first, not agent-first") is the authoring plane's change control expressed as a repository convention.

12.3Control plane and data plane

Generic If you have worked with networks or Kubernetes, the authoring plane is a control plane and the enforcement plane is a data plane, and the same engineering rules apply. They are worth stating because they decide the availability and latency of every agent you govern.

RuleWhyHow ARB does it
The control plane is never on the hot pathAn authoring tool, registry UI or GRC system being down must not stop agents. Policies are compiled and distributed ahead of time.The Rego bundle, params and catalog are loaded into the plane at build; the PDP is embedded (regopy), so a decision makes no network call
The data plane fails closed for writesIf the decision cannot be made, the act must not happenEvery PDP fact has a deny default; a contended state store refuses (StateUnavailableError)
Configuration is versioned and identifiableEvidence must say which rules decidedA configuration digest over manifest, required controls, catalog files, model pin and params; sign-off bound to it (CTL-0022)
The data plane reports, the control plane decides changeEnforcement must not rewrite its own rulesThe gate reads authored sign-offs and never infers them from green tests (D22)
The evidence path is append-only and cheap to writeIt runs on every call, so it must not become the bottleneck or the attack surfaceAllowlisted open log, free text in a separate detail store, hash chain with periodic anchors (Chapter 22)

The assurance plane has a different shape again: it is batch and analytical, run on demand or on a schedule, and it must be able to work from a fresh process with nothing but the evidence and the public keys. That property, "an auditor can verify without trusting the running system", is what separates assurance from monitoring.

12.4Ten capability layers

Notice that layer 3 has no control of its own in ARB. Tiering is the function that decides which controls apply, so it lives in the authoring plane and the gate, not on the run-time path. Layer 1 likewise is realised by process and fields rather than by a run-time control. A reference model that forced a run-time control into every layer would be decorative.

12.5Kinds of enforcement point

Your build Every control in ARB's catalog declares where it bites, in its enforcement_point field, and the field is a list because many controls bite in more than one place. The vocabulary has three values; a fourth, process, covers what code cannot do.

KindBites whenGood atBlind toARB catalog (29 entries)
Design-time gateBefore release, and on every changeFacts about the configuration: is it registered, pinned, signed off, are all required controls bound and provenAnything that depends on what the agent is asked or reads7 (e.g. CTL-0021, CTL-0022, CTL-0023, GBL-SOD-01)
Run-time PEPOn every callActs and their facts: limits, preconditions, identity, approval, injection boundary, budgetsWhether the configuration it runs is the approved one, unless told26 of 29
Evidence planeWhen the record is written and when it is verifiedMinimisation, tamper evidence, provenance, completenessPreventing the act; it proves, it does not refuse (except that a failed record refuses or reverses)4 (CTL-0009, CTL-0011, CTL-0019, GBL-EVID-01)

Two design observations follow from the distribution. First, the run-time PEP carries almost everything, which is why its complete mediation (Chapter 16) is the most important property in the system. Second, the controls that sit at two points are the interesting ones: separation of duties is checked at the gate (the control must be bound, proven by a conformance run and, at CRITICAL, carry no waiver before the agent may ship) and at run time (is this approver a different resolved subject from this executor, on this call?), and neither check can stand in for the other. The gate cannot see a particular approval; the PEP cannot see whether the deployed configuration is the one that was signed off.

Generic Inside "run-time PEP" there are several placements (in-process chokepoint, tool gateway, model gateway, data-access check, agent-to-agent edge, the system of record's own permissions), compared in Chapter 16. For a client estate, a useful artefact is a matrix of controls against placements per platform; that matrix is Capstone B's stage B3 (Chapter 53).

12.6Using the model

For assessment

Score each layer 0 to 5 (absent, ad hoc, defined, enforced, measured, optimised), with one piece of evidence required for any score above 2. The radar that results shows where to start, and the evidence rule stops the most common distortion, which is scoring documents as capabilities. A practical threshold: if inventory, identity, run-time action control or evidence score below 2, the organisation is not ready for any agent above the advisory level, whatever its other scores.

For architecture

For each control, decide its plane and its enforcement points, then its placement per platform. Every control should land in at least one enforcement point and the evidence plane; a control with no enforcement point is a policy statement, and one with no evidence cannot be audited.

For ownership

Assign every layer an accountable executive and every control an owner_role. ARB's RACI (docs/operating/RACI.md) shows the pattern: it is an overlay that groups the catalog's authored roles (Payments governor, Security, Platform/IAM, Data governance, Agent product owner, AI security, and the four roles the evolution phases added) under the functions an organisation actually has, without changing any control.

For the roadmap

Assumption The order that works for most estates: inventory, then tiering, then minimum run-time enforcement (identity and the PEP on writes), then evidence, then evaluation and conformance, then human oversight refinements, then operations. Policy runs in parallel from the start. The reasoning is that you cannot tier what you have not counted, cannot enforce proportionately without tiers, and cannot prove what you have not recorded; Chapter 51 turns this into a 90-day plan.

Waiting on the builds

Today both builds run the PDP embedded and the authoring plane as files in git, which is the simplest correct shape for one team. A hosted pm-agent or an org-bound ARB will need bundle distribution, a registry service and decision logs. When either lands, add the deployed control-plane topology (what is central, what is per-tenant, how bundles reach PEPs) as a figure, and revisit the "control plane is never on the hot path" row with measured numbers.

12.9In the room

In the room
  • For an executive: "Three questions run your programme: what must be true, what makes it true at the moment an agent acts, and how you prove it was true. Most organisations have the first, few have the second, almost none have the third."
  • For a CISO: "Twenty-six of our twenty-nine controls bite at run time. The design review decides which ones apply; it cannot be the control."
  • For an auditor: "Every control names its owner, its trigger, the evidence it leaves and the test that proves it fires, and the evidence can be verified without trusting the system that wrote it."

12.10Prove it

Defend cold · pairs with R04 and Capstone B1–B3
  1. RecallName the three planes, what each says, and the ARB directories for each.
  2. RecallState the five control-plane/data-plane rules and the ARB mechanism for each.
  3. ApplyPlace CTL-0005, CTL-0011 and CTL-0022 on the planes and enforcement points, and say what each placement can and cannot see.
  4. DesignA client runs Copilot Studio agents (no run-time hooks you control) and ADK agents on GCP. Draw the three planes for them and say which layers will score lowest on the Copilot side, and why.
  5. Trade-offEmbedded PDP versus a central policy service with sidecars: make the case for each at 10 agents and at 1,000.
