Standards, law and crosswalk engineering

The external texts a client will hold your design against, sorted by what each asks for: OWASP's agentic threat lists, NIST's risk framework and its new agent work, ISO/IEC 42001 and its family, the EU AI Act as amended by the 2026 Omnibus, and agent-specific guidance such as Singapore's. Then the engineering that connects them to a catalog without overclaiming: crosswalks as code, dated Verify cells and four numbers instead of one.

48.1Five kinds of external text

Generic Clients will put a stack of frameworks in front of you and ask whether the design "complies". The first move is to sort them, because each kind asks for a different thing and accepts different evidence.

KindExamplesWhat it asks of youEvidence it accepts
Threat taxonomyOWASP Top 10 for Agentic Applications; OWASP LLM Top 10; MITRE ATLAS; CSA MAESTROShow you considered each threat and what stops itA threat model, controls mapped per threat, red-team cases that exercise them
Risk frameworkNIST AI RMF and its profiles; ISO/IEC 23894Run a risk process: govern, map, measure, manageInventories, risk assessments, metrics, decisions with owners
Management systemISO/IEC 42001 (certifiable); ISO/IEC 42005 impact assessmentOperate a documented, audited system of policies, roles, controls and improvementPolicies, records, internal audits, management review; certification by an accredited body
Law and regulationEU AI Act; GDPR; sector rules (Chapter 49)Meet specific obligations by specific dates, depending on your role and the useTechnical documentation, logs, conformity assessment, registration, incident reports
Agent-specific guidanceSingapore's Model AI Governance Framework for Agentic AI; NIST's agent standards work; vendor frameworksAdopt recommended practices for autonomy, oversight and accessUsually your own: design records and operating evidence

None of these regulates "agents" as a category. They regulate threats, processes, organisations and uses (Chapter 11). The engineering consequence is that one well-built control catalog with operating evidence serves all five, provided each mapping says honestly how good it is. That is crosswalk engineering, 48.7.

48.2OWASP: the agentic threat lists

Generic Two OWASP documents matter, and they are easy to confuse. The Top 10 for Agentic Applications (version 2026, published 9 December 2025) names ten risks, ASI01 to ASI10, from agent goal hijack to rogue agents; Chapter 32 maps each to controls. The earlier Agentic AI Threats and Mitigations guide uses T-numbers (T3 privilege compromise, T8 repudiation, T9 identity spoofing, T12 agent communication poisoning, T13 rogue agents and so on). The general OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications (2025) still applies underneath: LLM01 prompt injection, LLM05 improper output handling, LLM06 excessive agency, LLM10 unbounded consumption.

Your build ARB's crosswalk cites LLM ids and the T-numbers; its posture document calls the T-number source the "Agentic Top 10", which is the other document. Adding an ASI column beside the T-numbers is finding F-07 (Appendix G). D44's check against the 2025 LLM list also caught a real error: CTL-0008 was mapped to LLM08, which in the 2025 list is "vector and embedding weaknesses", not identity; it now maps to Agentic T9.

Use a threat list to drive two things: the threat model (Chapter 32) and the red-team corpus. A threat with a mapped control but no red-team case is a claim, not a defence.

48.3NIST: the risk framework and the agent work

Generic The AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0, NIST AI 100-1, January 2023) organises AI risk work into four functions, GOVERN, MAP, MEASURE and MANAGE, each with categories and subcategories (MAP 3.5, MANAGE 4.1 and so on). The Generative AI Profile (NIST AI 600-1, July 2024) applies it to generative systems. Both are voluntary, freely published and widely used as the backbone of US enterprise AI policies.

NIST's agent-specific work is newer and mostly not final as of October 2026:

WorkStateWhy it matters for agents
AI Agent Standards Initiative, led by the Center for AI Standards and Innovation (CAISI), announced 17 February 2026Programme; a request for information on agent security (January 2026) and sector listening sessionsSignals where US voluntary standards for agent security and interoperability will come from
NCCoE concept paper on software and AI agent identity and authorisation (February 2026)Comment period closed; project work under way VerifyAgent identity, OAuth extensions, delegation chains and audit trails: the subjects of Chapter 18
Control overlays for securing AI systems (COSAiS) on SP 800-53Five use cases including single-agent and multi-agent systems; only one overlay (fine-tuning predictive AI) had a public draft by mid-2026 VerifyWill give federal and many regulated buyers a control baseline for agents, in a vocabulary their auditors already use
Cyber AI Profile (NIST IR 8596)Preliminary draft, December 2025 VerifyPlaces AI cyber risk in the Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 structure

Your build D44 checked every NIST hook against AI 100-1 and found that CTL-0004 and CTL-0005 had been mapped to MANAGE 2 ("benefits and impact management"), which has nothing to do with human oversight. The correct hooks are MAP 3.5 (processes for human oversight are defined and documented) for the approval gate and MANAGE 4.1 (post-deployment monitoring including appeal and override) for its measurement. The function-level tags elsewhere were left as they were, because adding subcategory numbers nobody checked would overstate the review.

48.4ISO/IEC 42001 and its family

Generic ISO/IEC 42001:2023 specifies an AI management system: requirements in clauses 4 to 10 (context, leadership, planning, support, operation, performance evaluation, improvement) and 38 reference controls in Annex A, with implementation guidance in Annex B. It is certifiable, and certification is a claim about an organisation's management system, not about any one agent. Its family fills in the pieces:

  • ISO/IEC 23894:2023, guidance on AI risk management.
  • ISO/IEC 42005:2025, AI system impact assessment.
  • ISO/IEC 42006:2025, requirements for bodies that audit and certify AI management systems.
  • ISO/IEC 27001 for information security, which most enterprises already hold and which carries the access-control and security controls 42001 does not.

Your build The standard's text is paid, so ARB's D44 verified its Annex A citations against a control-ID skeleton corroborated by two independent secondary sources, and said so. Five of eight labels mapped cleanly: operational controls A.9.2, operation and monitoring A.6.2.6, event logs A.6.2.8, verification and validation A.6.2.4, documentation for users A.8.2. Three did not: "human oversight", "access control" and "AI security" have no Annex A control by those names, and they stay [Verify] rather than be forced onto a clause that does not say that. Inventing an ISO/IEC 27001 family to house two of them was rejected as new scope. That is the right instinct: a crosswalk that cannot cite a clause should say so, not find the nearest number.

48.5The EU AI Act after the Omnibus

Generic Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 entered into force on 1 August 2024 and applies in stages. The Digital Omnibus on AI, Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, published in the Official Journal on 24 July 2026 and in force since 27 July 2026, moved the high-risk dates and changed several obligations.

What the Act means for an agent programme:

  • An agent is an AI system, classified by use. It is high-risk if its use falls in an Annex III area (employment, access to essential private and public services including credit scoring, education, law enforcement, migration, justice, critical infrastructure and others) or it is a safety component of a product under Annex I. A refund agent is not high-risk; an agent that screens job applicants or decides creditworthiness is.
  • Roles carry the duties. Providers carry most high-risk obligations (risk management, data governance, technical documentation, record-keeping in Article 12, transparency to deployers, human oversight by design in Article 14, accuracy and robustness, conformity assessment, registration). Deployers carry their own in Article 26: use per instructions, assign competent human oversight, monitor, keep the logs under their control for at least six months, inform affected workers. A firm that builds its own agent for its own use is both.
  • Transparency already applies. Under Article 50, people must be told they are interacting with an AI system unless it is obvious, and synthetic content must be marked; most of these duties apply from 2 August 2026.
  • General-purpose models are the provider's problem, mostly. GPAI model obligations (since 2 August 2025) fall on model providers; a firm that substantially modifies a model by fine-tuning should check whether it becomes one.
  • Serious incidents are reportable for high-risk systems (Article 73), which presumes you can reconstruct what happened: an evidence requirement (Chapter 22).

Your build D44 verified three EU mappings against the official text: Article 14 human oversight for the approval controls (CTL-0004, CTL-0005), Article 12 record-keeping for evidence minimisation, chaining and provenance (CTL-0009, CTL-0011, CTL-0019, with GDPR Article 5(1)(c) data minimisation), and Article 50 transparency for CTL-0015. The E5 additions (CTL-0025, CTL-0026) carry Article 50 as [Verify].

Gotcha

Article 12 asks high-risk systems to log events automatically over their lifetime for traceability; it does not ask for tamper evidence. Your evidence plane exceeds it, which is useful when a regulator, a court or an insurer asks whether a log could have been edited. Say "meets Article 12 and adds tamper evidence", not "Article 12 requires a hash chain".

48.6Agent-specific frameworks

Generic Guidance written for agents specifically is mostly principles-based. It is valuable because it names agent-specific practices (bounding autonomy, checkpoints for human approval, agent identity), and awkward for crosswalks because it has no numbered clauses to cite per control.

48.7Crosswalk engineering

Generic A crosswalk relates two vocabularies: your controls and someone else's requirements. Done in a spreadsheet, it drifts from the controls, hides how sure each cell is and collapses into a single coverage percentage. Done as code, it can be checked like any other artefact.

Your build ARB's practice, from D44 and D55:

  1. One source of truth per side. Each catalog control carries a standards block; governance/crosswalk/crosswalk.yaml holds the same cells; make gov-validate fails if the two differ.
  2. Every regulatory cell says how sure it is. EU AI Act and ISO cells carry [Verified <date>] with the article or clause named, or [Verify]. There is no third, bare state, and a test (test_no_crosswalk_cell_is_silently_bare_prose) enforces it. A [Verified] EU cell must name a real article, checked by pattern.
  3. Coverage has three statuses plus a qualifier. Against the client's 52-control pack (gcl_crosswalk.yaml), each row is implemented (named controls plus a test that proves it), mechanism (met by framework machinery, with a test that fails if the machinery is removed) or not-built (a reason only). Either of the first two may be partial, with what is absent.
  4. Four numbers, never one. At main on 19 October 2026: 10 implemented, 12 partial, 5 mechanism, 25 not-built, of 52. The evidence pack prints those four.
  5. Findings flow both ways. A build control with no matching pack control gets a pack_gap: CTL-0006 (idempotent writes) has none in the pack, which is a finding about the pack, sent to the team that owns it. The build does not edit the pack.
  6. Corrections are recorded, not silently fixed. D44's two corrections are named in the affected catalog files and tested so the old values cannot return.

Generic The test for a crosswalk is the auditor's question "show me". If a cell says "implemented", the answer must be a test or a piece of evidence, not a paragraph. That rule alone removes most of what makes compliance mappings untrustworthy.

48.8Writing Verify cells honestly

  • Verify against the primary text, or say what you verified against instead (a corroborated skeleton for a paid standard, as D44 did).
  • Date every verified cell. Standards are revised and laws amended; the Omnibus moved dates eleven months before they applied.
  • Leave a cell open rather than force it. "No Annex A control by this name" is a finding. A nearest-clause citation is an error waiting for an auditor.
  • Match the granularity you checked. Function-level NIST tags that were confirmed stay function-level.
  • Keep law and guidance separate. A voluntary framework's recommendation is not an obligation; mark the difference in the report.
  • Re-verify on a trigger. A new version of a standard, an amendment, a new delegated act or guideline, or a quarterly review.

48.10In the room

In the room
  • "Are we compliant with the EU AI Act?" "That depends on what each agent is used for and whether you build or deploy it. Most agents are not high-risk; transparency duties already apply to all of them. For any agent in an Annex III area, high-risk duties now start on 2 December 2027, and the logging and oversight we build today is most of the evidence you will need."
  • "Can you map us to NIST, ISO and OWASP?" "Yes, from one control catalog. Each mapping says whether it is verified against the source, and each claim of coverage points to a test or a record. We report what is implemented, partial, provided by the platform and not built, separately."
  • "Will ISO 42001 certification cover our agents?" "It certifies the management system you run them under, which auditors and customers value. It does not certify any agent's behaviour; the operating evidence does that."
Check before quoting

Confirm EU AI Act dates against the consolidated text including Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 and any later guidelines or delegated acts; confirm which NIST agent documents are final; quote OWASP ids with the document name and version; and never quote ISO/IEC 42001 clause titles you have not checked. Sector rules are in Chapter 49.

Waiting on the builds

When ARB adds an ASI column, replace the T-number discussion in the OWASP section with its mapping. When NIST publishes the single-agent and multi-agent COSAiS overlays, add a worked mapping of ARB's catalog to them. When the Commission's guidelines on high-risk classification are final, add a worked classification of three agent use cases.
