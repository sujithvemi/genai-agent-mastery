PART III · CHAPTER 14

The control as a contract

A control is a promise with terms: what risk it removes, the exact condition under which it must act, who answers for it, what record proves it acted, how a test shows it can, and what happens when someone wants an exception. Written that way, every field has a consumer, and a control that cannot keep its promise is visible.

14.1What a control is, and what it is not

Generic Organisations write three kinds of statement and call all of them controls. A principle ("AI will be used responsibly") sets direction and cannot be tested. A policy statement ("refunds above a threshold require approval") states a requirement and can be audited only by sampling. A control is a mechanism that makes a policy statement true, with enough attached to it that anyone can check whether it does. The difference is not wording; it is whether someone could build it, test it and prove it from the text alone.

The standard control-design checklist (objective, trigger, owner, evidence, test method, exception path) exists to force that. Each field answers a question someone downstream will ask:

FieldQuestion it answersWho asks
ObjectiveWhich risk does this remove?Risk owner, auditor, the next engineer deciding whether it can go
TriggerUnder exactly what condition must it act?The engineer writing the rule; the tester writing the case
OwnerWho answers for it and approves changes to it?Risk committee, incident commander
EvidenceWhich record proves it acted?Auditor, investigator
Test methodHow do we show it can act?The gate, the conformance suite, the red team
Exception pathHow is it waived, by whom, for how long, and when never?Delivery teams under pressure; the risk committee

14.2Anatomy of a control file

Your build ARB authors each control as a YAML file in governance/catalog/, validated against a JSON Schema (2020-12) in governance/_schemas/control.schema.yaml. D16 chose JSON Schema over Python models deliberately: the schema file is itself an artifact an auditor reads, the field specification made executable. Here is separation of duties, complete:

id: CTL-0005
title: Separation of duties
objective: Requester ≠ approver of a write
trigger: approver == executor principal
owner_role: Security
evidence: approver identity ≠ executor in evidence
test_method: "same-principal approve → blocked"
exception_path: none (GBL-SOD-01)
min_tier: HIGH
applies_when: "action_class ≥ reversible-write"
enforcement_point:
  - design-time gate
  - runtime PEP
standards:
  nist_ai_rmf: "MAP-3 human oversight process (3.5); MANAGE-4 override/appeal (4.1)"
  iso_42001: "[Verify] human oversight"
  owasp: null
  eu_ai_act: "[Verified 2026-09-05] Art. 14 human oversight"
gap_ref: GAP-05
status: ratified

Every field has at least one consumer in the build, which is what makes the file a contract rather than documentation. The figure maps them.

validate.py adds the checks a schema cannot express: id equals the filename; every applies_when names a real axis and value (the bridge to the tiering engine); gap_ref values are unique and resolve. D16 kept this as its own make target and CI step, gov-validate, separate from make check, so "the catalog is well-formed" reads as a distinct signal from "the code is green".

14.3Writing the trigger

Generic The trigger is the field most often written badly, because it is where policy language meets code. A good trigger is a predicate over facts the system can establish at the moment of the act. Compare:

WeakWhy it failsStrong
"Appropriate human oversight of high-impact actions"Neither "appropriate" nor "high-impact" is a fact the system has"Any call to a tool whose action class ≥ reversible-write, at tier ≥ HIGH, without a verified approval token bound to this call's arguments"
"Refunds must be reasonable"No threshold, no reference value"amount_minor > order.total_minor − refunded_minor, read from the order system"
"The agent should not be manipulated"Describes an outcome, not a condition"A tool result from an untrusted source reaches a decision input without being demoted to a data channel"

Your build ARB enforces traceability in both directions. Every rule in tool_call.rego must be a mechanical translation of one control's trigger (the bundle header says that a rule which cannot be traced back to an authored trigger does not belong, and escalates). And since D30, every rule must also be reachable: every fact it reads must have a producer in the PIP, which tests/test_no_dead_policy.py checks mechanically (Chapter 16). Traceable is necessary; reachable is the other half.

14.4Evidence and test method

Generic "Evidence" in a control is not "we log it". It is the specific record whose existence, content and integrity prove the control acted on a particular occasion. For separation of duties, it is the approver's resolved identity and the executor's, both in the record, and different. An auditor should be able to read the evidence field and write the query.

"Test method" is not "we have tests". It is the specific case that shows the control can act. Good test methods have two halves: a positive case (the bad act is refused) and a falsifier (remove or disable the mechanism, and the positive case must now fail). Without the falsifier, a passing test may be passing for an unrelated reason.

Your build D56 is the cautionary tale. Applying the rule "every proof is checked by disabling what it proves", the build disabled three mechanisms one at a time and ran the full suite of 912 tests. All three survived. One was the golden-set scorer: since P1, "golden 25/25" had been reported at every phase tag, yet no test had ever shown the scorer rejecting a wrong outcome. A scorer returning nothing would have scored every case as passing. The number was true; nothing showed it could have been false. D56 authorised falsifier tests for all three, and the rule is now standing: a crosswalk row marked implemented or mechanism (D55) needs a test that fails when the mechanism is removed.

Gotcha

A test that a control fires and a test that a specific mechanism made it fire are different tests. pm-agent hit this in P4 (D-PM-P4-n): removing the approver-versus-executor comparison passed every test, because every separation-of-duties probe self-approved with the service account, which a different rule also refused, under the same control id. The fix required each self-approval row to be refused by the SoD comparison itself, identified by reason, not just by control id. Attribute refusals to the mechanism, not only to the control.

14.5The exception path

Generic Every real control programme has exceptions; the question is whether they are designed or improvised. A designed exception path names who may grant a waiver, for how long, with what compensating measure, and where the waiver is recorded so the gate can see it. It also names where no waiver is possible.

Your build Three rules from the builds are worth adopting anywhere:

  • Some controls have no exception path at the top tier. At CRITICAL, approval on writes, separation of duties and GBL-SOD-01 are non-waivable; the gate blocks an agent that carries an active waiver on any of them (Chapter 13).
  • A waiver never makes a control met. pm-agent's D-PM-P4-j considered letting a waiver satisfy its control and rejected it as a new governance rule: waivers are reported, they expire, and at CRITICAL an active waiver on the non-waivable set blocks. The gate's "met" stays a technical fact (bound and proven).
  • Accepting residual risk is a human act, never inferred. Executive sign-off is an authored, signed record, and the gate refuses to infer it from a green run (D22). A test pins it: everything enforced, everything proven, sign-off withdrawn, gate blocked.

14.6Fields that admit limits

Your build The most distinctive thing about ARB's control schema is a family of optional fields whose only job is to state, inside the control, what the control does not do. They were added one at a time as the red team found limits, and each comes with a test that keeps the statement true.

FieldStatesOnOrigin
detection_windowA detective control's blind spot and its size, by naming the params key that sets it (never restating the number)CTL-0011 (truncation detected only back to the last anchor), and six E-phase controls (CTL-0020–0023, 0025, 0026)D30, D34
deferred_scopeThe part of the objective the active plane does not cover, why, the preserved spec of the deferred half, and when it landsCTL-0012 (the stated-currency half)D30, D36
human_factorsWhere the real failure is human (approval fatigue), the authored friction against it and its params keyCTL-0004D31, D35
classifierThat a model-based classifier sits on the path, that it is monotone-restrictive, what it does when unavailable (hold or deny, never pass), and which eval reports its accuracyCTL-0025, CTL-0026D52, D54

The schema's own description of detection_window carries the principle: absent means the control makes no such exclusion, not that it has none, and an undeclared blind spot is worse than a declared one because only the second can be reasoned about. When you design a client catalog, include these fields from day one. An auditor who finds a limit you documented trusts the rest of the catalog more; one who finds a limit you didn't trusts none of it.

14.7Derivable and declared halves

Your build D23 drew a distinction that applies to most controls on writes. Some of a control can be derived by the system from trusted state; some needs the caller to declare something the system then checks. For the currency control: the derivable half is "the refund's currency is the order's currency, and an order that cannot be resolved denies"; the declared half is "the caller states a currency, and a disagreement with the order is caught". The derivable half removes the hazard (the system never guesses a currency). The declared half only adds detection of a confused caller.

D23 also set a preference that generalises: provable-preventive over inferable-detective. Its other ruling in the same entry, on evidence minimisation, chose structural separation (free text never enters the open log; it goes wholesale to an access-controlled store and only its hash stays) over an entity recogniser that tries to spot names. The first can be asserted by a test that holds by construction; the second is a detector with an error rate. When a control can be made true by structure, do that, and keep detection for what structure cannot reach.

14.8The posture ladder

Your build A control's state is not binary. ARB's build story uses a ladder, and each rung is a different claim with different evidence:

RungClaimEvidence that earns it
UnguardedThe risk exists and is knownA gap with a location and a demonstrating test (CONTROL_GAPS.md)
SpecifiedA control is defined and required where it appliesA schema-valid catalog entry; the gate blocks while it is unmet
EnforcedCode refuses the bad actA binding plus a passing conformance check against the real plane, generated this run (D27)
VerifiedIt held under attackA red-team corpus run with each finding closed by a regression test, residuals named (D30)
PortableIt holds on any bindingThe same decisions across fake, local and cloud bindings; a firewall test (D32)
FleetIt generalisesA different archetype derives a different set from the unchanged catalog (D39)
Operable and defensiblePeople can run and explain itRunbooks, RACI, governance SLOs, a defence pack (D45, D46)

The ladder is the honest answer to "is this agent governed?": ask which rung each required control is on. In client work it is also a roadmap shape: most estates have controls at "specified" and need to climb, not to write more.

14.9Growing a catalog

Your build Four practices kept ARB's catalog coherent from 15 controls to 26 plus 3 invariants:

  1. Gap-first. Every control traces to a measured gap (gap_ref), and every gap to a file, a line and a demonstrating test. Nothing enters the catalog because a framework lists it; framework coverage is a crosswalk question (Chapter 48).
  2. Author once, bind per shape. A control is defined once; its bindings realise it for each agent shape. D39 rejected "read variants" of controls because they would fork one control into two and reintroduce agent-fitting.
  3. Re-scope honestly rather than retire silently. When CTL-0012's declared half could not be reached, D36 removed the unreachable rule and the fact it read together, recorded the deferred half in deferred_scope with its preserved text, and kept the derived half enforced. The conformance count stayed 18/18, and D36 explains why that is right: the control lost the appearance of coverage it never had, not coverage.
  4. Ratification is a human act on the set. D49 flipped all 22 files from draft to ratified on your word, and states what it does not claim: ratifying the catalog's design is not a claim that the build is production-ready.
Waiting on the builds

CTL-0027, the fairness control, is authored but held outside the active catalog (docs/governance/held/) because its criterion cannot separate noise from bias (D74). When it is either ratified with a defensible criterion or retired, add it to the honesty-fields table (its fairness_digest_inputs field is the first that binds a measurement artifact to configuration) and to the ladder discussion as the worked example of a control that stopped at "specified" on purpose.

14.12In the room

In the room
  • "We have a control library of 200 items." Ask how many have a trigger a system can evaluate, and for how many a test would fail if the mechanism were removed. The honest count is usually small, and that is the starting point, not a criticism.
  • "Our framework lists all the right controls." Listing is the specified rung. Ask for the enforced and verified rungs for the five highest-risk agents.
  • On limits: "Every control we ship states its own blind spot where it has one. You will find our limits in the catalog, not in an incident report."
